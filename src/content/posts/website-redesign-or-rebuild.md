“We need a new website” can mean a visual refresh, new content, better mobile layouts, a new platform, or custom functionality. Those are different scopes with different costs and risks. Before choosing a redesign or rebuild, identify the problems you need to solve and check whether your current site can support the change.

## Start with what is not working

Collect examples rather than relying on a general feeling that the site is old. Is it hard to navigate? Does it fail on mobile? Are key service pages missing? Is the content inaccurate? Do forms or integrations fail? Are routine updates difficult because of the current platform? Ask staff and a few customers where they encounter friction.

Separate user-facing issues from technical constraints. A page that looks cluttered may need clearer content and layout, while an obsolete plugin or unsupported platform may require technical work. Some sites have both. Rank needs by their effect on customers and the business, then define the outcome you want.

## When a redesign may be enough

A focused redesign can make sense when the site’s underlying platform, page structure, and integrations remain suitable. You may be able to improve typography, layout, navigation, calls to action, and page content without replacing the whole system. This can preserve familiar workflows and reduce the number of moving parts that change at once.

Before deciding, confirm that the current theme and implementation can support the new design, mobile behavior, accessibility needs, and content updates. A visual refresh that leaves the same broken form or slow editorial process will not solve the underlying problem.

## When a rebuild may be justified

A rebuild deserves consideration when the current platform cannot support required functionality, the theme or code is difficult to maintain, the site structure blocks important content, or the implementation is no longer a safe foundation. A rebuild may also be needed when changing platforms or replacing custom features that have no viable path forward.

The word “rebuild” should not mean starting without a plan. Inventory existing URLs, content, images, forms, integrations, and user accounts. Decide what to keep, rewrite, redirect, or retire. Identify who owns the domain, hosting account, and source files. Estimate the work for data migration and testing as well as design and development.

## Protect search visibility and continuity

If page URLs change, list old and new addresses and plan redirects where appropriate. Update internal links and test important pages before launch. Google’s guidance explains that crawling and indexing depend on access to pages and links; a new site should make important content discoverable and avoid accidental blocking. Do not promise that a redesign will improve rankings on its own.

Preserve business-critical workflows: contact submissions, payments, booking, memberships, and notifications. Test them on staging and again after launch. Make a backup before major migration work and agree on a fallback plan if an issue appears.

## Compare proposals by scope

Ask each provider to describe what is included: content strategy, design review, development, migration, redirects, integrations, testing, training, and post-launch support. Ask what is not included and how additional pages or features are handled. A lower initial quote may cover fewer tasks, so compare deliverables rather than labels like “redesign package.”

## Decision summary

Choose a redesign when the foundations are sound and the needed change is primarily content, layout, or presentation. Choose a rebuild when the current foundation blocks the business requirements or cannot be maintained sensibly. If the evidence is mixed, begin with an assessment and a prioritized scope before committing to either.
